# Operational definition of a place of worship

Version: 0.1.7. Date: 2026-11-16. Status: adopted working operational definition. This dated snapshot preserves version 0.1.7. The [canonical operational definition](../operational-definition.md) presents the current version.

**At a specified time, a place of worship is a reproducibly mappable site for which source evidence supports recurring religious worship by or for a community.**

## Purpose and entity

A definition serves a purpose. This project asks what sites of collective worship do for the communities who express religious piety there, in one form or another, and through them for the societies around them. Those are functional questions. Answering a functional question requires consistent counting of such sites across places and times.

For our interests and purposes, a place of worship (PoW) is an entity described by six fundamental kinds of attribute. Spatial attributes indicate where it is and at what precision. Temporal attributes indicate when it existed, when worship recurred, and when it changed. Worship attributes indicate which recurring worship occurs and how often. Community attributes indicate by or for whom. Functional attributes indicate what the site does for that community, the matter the project exists to study. Relational attributes link a PoW to organisations and congregations, to the places of worship that preceded and succeeded it, and to the components of a "coherent worship complex". Every attribute has two kinds of imprecision, which the record keeps apart: uncertainty, where evidence about a matter of fact is incomplete, and vagueness, where the boundary of the concept itself is indistinct, as in how often worship must recur or where an entity begins and ends. Better evidence reduces uncertainty; a stated rule reduces inherent vagueness.

A unique PoW identifier tracks the identity of a PoW through time. Attributes change; this identifier, however, perdures. The identifier changes only when the entity changes. The section on identity states when we take this to happen, given our interests and purposes. In the [2026-09-22 discussion of counting places and coherent worship complexes](pow-counting-and-complexes-discussion-2026-09-22.md) we work through examples. Version 0.1.6 of our definition adopted that discussion's rule for replacement venues and shared rooms; the boundary of a "coherent worship complex" remains open in our linked discussion.

## Criteria

Again, recalling that uncertainty is permissible, we say a site meets our definition of a PoW when source evidence supports *all* of the following three criteria:

1. **Reproducibly mappable site.** Preserved location evidence allows investigators to identify the same physical place at the spatial precision required by the study. The site may be a building, parcel, compound, room, adapted or shared venue, temporary structure, or natural place. A vehicle or other mobile venue is not a site; the stop it returns to may be, as the section on identity states (ruling, 2026-11-16).
2. **Recurring religious worship.** Evidence supports repeated or established worship use. Weekly services, seasonal practice, and an established annual pilgrimage may each qualify. Uncertain recurrence remains provisional. For the census-year derivation, a recorded period of worship at any known frequency establishes the place as in use for the years it covers, and each such year also carries a level of use: regular (weekly or more, monthly, or several times a year) or intermittent (annual or occasional). Only a period whose frequency of use is uncertain covers its years without establishing use; those years derive as uncertain until a reviewer decides on the evidence (rulings R-F1 and R-F1′, 2026-09-03). A period states whether it covers its years throughout or places worship somewhere within them: "the 1920s" may mean either, and only the first establishes each year, while the second derives every year of the decade as uncertain until narrowed (ruling, 2026-09-22).
3. **Worship by or for a community.** Worship occurs by or for a congregation, resident religious community, or defined institutional population. *For* admits worship conducted on a community's behalf: a chaplaincy serving a hospital, prison, or military population, or a ritual specialist performing worship for a community that does not assemble as a congregation. The criterion therefore does not presuppose that the community is the agent of worship. Restricted access is compatible with community use.

Private devotion, religious affiliation, sacred significance, religious architecture, an organisation's address, and a one-off ceremony are candidate evidence assessed against all three criteria. When we describe "evidence" we mean public evidence. Privacy, access, and cultural rules govern collection, storage, and publication. We respect privacy. The project is at an early stage and has not yet been able to consult the communities whose places it classifies, so for now we rule on every community's sites by the same criteria. Each such ruling is provisional and open to revision on the advice of the community concerned (recorded 2026-11-16). **That a place cannot meet our standard of reproducible evidence does not entail that it is not a community's place of worship.** Our definition is relative to our interests and purposes, which are inevitably limited.


Two kinds of site call for a stated default ruling: cemeteries and homes. We allow both kinds to enter our record as *candidate* PoWs from any source. Both may become places of worship only when evidence shows recurring worship by or for a community there. Thus we apply our criteria to these types consistently. Silence in the sources, as when OpenStreetMap enters a cemetery as a PoW, leaves the site uncertain; the definition sets no default inclusion by tradition or by site type, since such a default would judge identity by a silent heuristic, which the project forbids. A household is not a community for criterion 3: a family altar or a household shrine is private devotion, however regular, and does not establish a place of worship. A corporate kin group worshipping at a shared site, a lineage, clan, gens, or hapū, is a community for criterion 3; the household exclusion reaches only the domestic unit worshipping for itself. For us, the test between the two types (private and public) is residence: a household denotes one co-resident domestic unit, whatever its size. Yet the community condition for criterion 3 spans more than one unit. A site therefore counts when worship there is by or for people of more than one household. It would not count when its worshippers are restricted to the place's residents. A home counts when it hosts worship by or for a community beyond the household, as a house church does, or as a great house does when the district gathers there. A cemetery counts when recurring rites for a community occur there, at whatever frequency the evidence supports, and fails criterion 2 when it serves burial and secular commemoration alone (ruling, 2026-09-22).

Our household exclusion reflects a decision about a community unit. As such our rationale should be on record. Fustel de Coulanges describes the religion of the ancient city as lodged first in the hearth cult of each household and then in the shared cults of the gens, the phratry, and the city. Hearth worship of that kind is religion. Such religion, according to Fustel, was socially consequential at the origins of the ancient city. In our work, this unit is excluded as a PoW because a household is not a community location in the project's sense: were every household with a shrine a site, the count of places of worship in a city would equal the count of its households and would measure population rather than the organisation of religion. What domestic worship is, for the project's questions, is a property of a population, the prevalence of worship at home in a community at a time. The shift Fustel describes, from a religion located in households to one located in shared sites, is measurable only if domestic worship were recorded at that level, as a documented attribute of a community or an area beside the count of its shared sites. Recorded as sites this unit would be invisible, drowned, so to speak, in its own denominator. Moreover, public, reproducible location evidence for private hearths does not exist for the ancient city. Presently, it would be forbidden to collect for living households. As such, what the sources describe is evidence about the institution, which the record can hold about a population yet cannot hold as a site (ruling, 2026-09-22). This case illustrates something of the necessary arbitrariness in defining a PoW. Properties of things should not depend on whether those properties can be observed, at least not at the level of ordinary middle-sized dry goods (i.e. the physics people inhabit). However without defining measurable units we cannot count, and we need consistent counting for the science this work hopes to eventually support.


## Identity over time

We think of a place of worship as one entity at one site. A PoW's identifier follows the place of worship even as its attributes may change. For example it keeps its identifier through change in its fabric, its organisations, its communities, and its activity. A building burnt down and rebuilt, restored, enlarged, or moved within the site is, for us, the same place of worship. However we mark changes in its spatial and other attributes over time. The site is the unit at the spatial precision the study requires (criterion 1). A building shifted within its parcel or compound therefore stays within the same site. A building that is picked up and shipped overseas, and set up for worship in a new setting is not the same place of worship. Note there is inherent vagueness in what attributes count as change sufficient to call a PoW the same PoW over time. Recall that we are interested in consistent counting. Yet edge cases are inevitable. A PoW moved across five paddocks to a new owner and set up for another denomination might reasonably demand a new identifier. One moved the same distance to a new owner, but retaining its denomination, is a vague case that demands a rule. One that is not moved but undergoes such a change in ownership and denomination might also be considered a vague case in need of a rule. Generally ruling on vague cases would not affect counts of PoWs in a region but might have implications for longitudinal analyses. For us, a change of religion, denomination, congregation, or organisation while worship continues at the site is dated as a change in its community attributes. For us, inactivity is an attribute too: a place whose worship has ceased while its site stands and no other use has claimed it is inactive, keeps its identifier, and switches back to active if worship resumes; otherwise it remains dormant. We expect that investigators will use worship recurrence rates (and their confidence levels) as the relevant functional indicators for a PoW. Having a site identifier is not sufficient for thinking a PoW remains active. However, it is sufficient for thinking that, according to the evidence as we judged it, the site was at some time used by or for a community for recurring worship.

For us, a place of worship ends (and is not merely dormant) when its recurring worship ceases and the site is destroyed, repurposed, or abandoned, with no community continuing or succeeding to its worship use there. Whether a long gap is an inactive period or an end is a reviewer's recorded judgement on the evidence, with the basis and the alternative kept. The definition does not set a duration threshold. For our purposes, a later recurrence of worship at the same location, by a community without any succession to the earlier use, is a new place of worship with its own identifier, linked to the earlier one by location (think of the Cathedral of Mexico, built over the Templo Mayor). Hence, for us, location alone is never sufficient to establish identity: the ground is a spatial attribute, and a place of worship is more than its ground (ruling, 2026-09-22).

For us, a move to a different site initiates a new place of worship, noting again the inherent vagueness of "move." A site that is lifted and shuffled a few metres (or across five paddocks) serving the same community would not generally count as a new site. A place moved across town might well do. In such a case, the place at the new site would have a unique identifier, linked to its predecessor as a relational attribute, and the entity at the old site, if one indeed remained there, would stay in the record, recorded as inactive or ended (ruling, 2026-09-22). The community's continuity lives in the link; the identifier stays with the site. Paddock shuffling within the same parcel aside, we rule that any move to a distinct property requires a unique identifier. The identifier at the destination differs from the one at the origin, but it is new only when the destination holds no place of worship already. A community that moves to a site where the record already holds a place of worship, active or inactive, and succeeds to its worship use there, becomes a further community of that place; the link to the community's former site records the move (see the Transitional Cathedral among the worked examples).

A place of worship is neither a building nor an organisation. An organisation may conduct worship in many places; each mappable place of collective worship is considered its own unique unit; organisations and congregations are recorded as linked entities. A building or compound may hold several places of worship, each at its own site within it. Think of a hospital with a chapel and a separate prayer room. A room shared by several communities, at the same hours or at different ones, would on our pragmatic definition count as a single place of worship with several community attributes.

Nor is a place of worship a vehicle. A vehicle or other mobile venue carries worship from place to place, and its changing position gives investigators no fixed place to find again, so under criterion 1 a vehicle is not a site. Where worship by or for a community recurs at a stop the vehicle returns to, the stop is the site and may be a place of worship, and we record the vehicle as a linked entity, as we record an organisation. A vehicle with no recurring stop yields no place of worship, however regular its worship; its activity is evidence about the organisation that runs it (ruling, 2026-11-16).

To count as a PoW, there must at some point in time be evidence (with uncertainty) for recurring collective worship. On religionmap.org at present, most PoW information has been sourced from OpenStreetMap. This indicates that someone thought, perhaps erroneously, that a location had the OpenStreetMap attribute for place of worship, which is ill defined. As such the evidence for religionmap.org at present is, taken as a whole, uncertain. Moreover, at any given time, whether worship at a location continues at an earlier place of worship is an inference from evidence about the site, the community, and its practice. OpenStreetMap gives uncertain and inconsistent evidence for temporal bounds.

**Our project therefore records identity as an explicit, revisable review decision. Our review systems require a stated basis for decisions about the attributes claimed for the locations on the map.**

### A comment on geography

We use the term "mappable" to denote geography. However, there are two complications. The geography is typically uncertain for historic places. Streets are built or renamed. Towns empty. Civilisations fall to ruins and others replace them. However, fortunately, longitude and latitude remain constant. To handle spatial uncertainty we allow a place to have spatial uncertainty bands around any point, as well as temporal uncertainty bands (which we have yet to figure out how to visually represent, even as the data are collected).

We note further that a point may not be precise enough. Within one plotted point we count one place of worship per distinct worship space, unless the spaces are components of one coherent worship complex, in which case the complex counts once. Two communities with their own rooms are the ordinary evidence that there are two spaces; one room used by two communities is one space with two community attributes. So two distinct hospital chapels known only to the same hospital centroid remain two places of worship drawn at the same point. We say that identity requires evidence about the places the point represents (recorded 2026-09-22). The rationale is not that this carves nature at a joint. It is our interest and purpose in tracking social consequences: the activity of sharing a place of worship differs from the activity of keeping separate ones, and the count should register that difference.

### Worked examples

Here we offer some worked examples to clarify the (inevitably arbitrary) rules that govern vague cases, recalling that uncertainty requires a distinct parameter for each attribute. Each example states the arrangement, the count of identifiers, and the rule that supports our classification. These are the cases reviewers will encounter, and the cases most often counted wrongly.

- **ChristChurch Cathedral and the Transitional Cathedral, Christchurch.** Two. The cathedral in Cathedral Square is one place of worship, inactive since the 2011 earthquakes, active again if its community returns after the reinstatement or another community succeeds to worship there, and ended only if no community does and the site is destroyed, repurposed or abandoned. The Transitional Cathedral, known as the Cardboard Cathedral and opened in August 2013, stands at Latimer Square on the site of St John the Baptist Church, consecrated in 1865 and demolished after the February 2011 earthquake. The place at Latimer Square therefore begins with St John's, not in 2013. St John's parish, which held the site, gave it for the Transitional Cathedral on the understanding that the building would pass to the parish once the cathedral in the Square is reinstated; in 2023 the diocese dissolved the parish, most of whose congregation had voted in 2018 to leave the diocese, and transferred its responsibilities to the cathedral. The cathedral's worship at Latimer Square thus succeeds to the parish's use, so the place has not ended: the years between the demolition and the opening are an inactive period, the cardboard building is a new structure on a continuing site, and the cathedral congregation is a second community there, linked to the inactive cathedral in the Square. Both places count while both are in use, and the Latimer Square place counts for as long as worship continues there, whether or not the cathedral in the Square reopens.
- **Hagia Sophia, Istanbul.** One. A church from the fourth century, a Latin church from 1204 to 1261, Orthodox again to 1453, a mosque to 1935, a museum to 2020, and a mosque since. Each conversion continued worship at the site under a new community and is a dated change of community and worship attributes; the museum years are an inactive period; one identifier throughout.
- **The Templo Mayor and the Metropolitan Cathedral, Mexico City.** Two. The Templo Mayor was enlarged and rebuilt over its earlier stages some seven times between the fourteenth century and 1521: one place of worship with dated structures, orientations, and rituals. Its worship ended with its destruction after the conquest. The cathedral, begun in 1573 within the Aztec sacred precinct and built in part from the temple's stone, is a new place of worship with no succession to the earlier use, born across the decades of its construction and dated as an interval. The reused fabric and the shared ground are relational attributes, and neither makes the two places one.
- **A building moved within its site.** One. A church shifted within its parcel is the same place of worship; the move is a dated spatial attribute.
- **A room that passes from Catholic to shared use.** One. The change of community is dated; the identifier stays.
- **A hospital prayer room shared by Muslims and Catholics, at the same hours or at different ones.** One, multi-faith, with the communities, rituals, and schedules as attributes. Private prayer and last rites conducted there are worship attributes of that place. An isolated bedside rite elsewhere in the hospital is evidence of activity to assess against criterion 2, and by itself no place.
- **A hospital with a distinct Catholic chapel and a distinct Muslim prayer room.** Two. Each room is its own site within the building; the hospital's roof and ownership unite nothing.
- **A cathedral whose nave serves the diocese and whose side chapel serves a Polish congregation.** One. Both spaces are components of the cathedral, and the second community is a community attribute of the complex. The same two communities in a hospital's separate chapel and prayer room count two, because there the spaces are separately established inside a facility whose purpose is not worship.
- **A hospital chaplaincy suite with a chapel and a prayer room off one lobby, run as one spiritual-care centre.** One. The spaces are organised together as a worship precinct, so they form a coherent worship complex within the hospital, with two community attributes. This is the boundary case a hospital reviewer meets first, and the judgement is recorded with its basis.
- **A cathedral with several altars, a convent with several worship rooms, a worship campus with several buildings, or three adjacent temple buildings forming one coherent worship complex.** One each. The components and their differing rituals and recurrence are attributes of the complex, recorded when the source distinguishes them and never required at entry. Recorded component activity keeps its scope and is never summed, since one ceremony can appear under several components; the complex counts at most once at an observation time.
- **Two adjacent, independently established places of worship.** Two. Adjacency, shared ownership, and a common roof establish no complex. Where a coherent complex ends remains open, and the [counting discussion](pow-counting-and-complexes-discussion-2026-09-22.md) works through it.
- **Two distinct hospital chapels known only to the same hospital centroid.** Two, drawn at one point, as above.
- **A municipal cemetery where families keep Obon, Qingming, All Souls or Día de Muertos rites.** One, with intermittent use. The rites recur on a calendar the community keeps, so the cemetery meets criteria 2 and 3 and derives present at the intermittent level under R-F1′; each study's threshold decides whether it counts. A cemetery used only for burial and secular commemoration is no place of worship. Rites such as mizuko kuyō, performed at a temple by individuals visiting separately over time, count through the temple as asynchronous community use of one site.
- **A churchyard or a temple cemetery.** None of its own. The burial ground is a component of the coherent worship complex and never counts separately.
- **A "Sukkah Mobile", a vehicle that invites passers-by to "Come Make a Blessing on the Lulav".** Parked at the same corner through Sukkot each year: one, at the corner, with intermittent use, and the vehicle recorded as a linked entity. The visitors come separately to make the blessing, which is asynchronous community use of one site, as at the temple where mizuko kuyō is performed. Roaming with no fixed stop: none. A vehicle is not a site, and its activity is evidence about the organisation that runs it.
- **A home.** One when it is a house church: an apartment where a congregation meets, or a room used weekly by a fellowship, with the identifier attached to the dwelling as a site and privacy rules governing publication. None when it holds a family altar or a household shrine, because the household is not a community for criterion 3, whatever the regularity of its devotion.
- **An ancestral hall, a lineage's citang at Qingming, a gens' tomb at the Parentalia, a marae's urupā.** One each. A corporate kin group beyond one household is a community for criterion 3, the rites recur, and the site is mappable. A reviewer who reads "household" as reaching these has misread the rule.
- **The household hearth: a lararium, a butsudan, a Hindu home shrine.** None as a site. Where documented for a population, domestic worship is recorded as an attribute of that community or area, so that the balance between worship at home and worship at shared sites can be studied. A large household is judged by the residence test: a joint-family compound of dozens whose worshippers are its residents alone is one household and counts none, however many they are; a Roman familia whose non-resident clients join its cult, or a great house where the district gathers, is a place, because worship there is by or for people of more than one household. A resident lineage group is therefore counted by the same test as everyone else: its compound is a household, and its ancestral hall, used by the lineage's several households, is a place.

## Measurement over time

The analytical unit is a place of worship's time-indexed **worship-function state**. Record physical existence, worship use, location, organisations, congregations, timing, and uncertainty as distinct claims. The `site_id` identifies the place of worship as an entity; dated occupancy records give the years its site was in use, and geometry history dates changes within the site.

Source records, OpenStreetMap objects, and field observations provide evidence about the worship-function state. Reviewer acceptance establishes supported claims as recorded states and longitudinal events. An accepted record may preserve a time-bounded history after worship use ends. Preserve the source or community description alongside any project classification.

**Our purpose here is to consistently record evidence for attributes of PoWs**.

**We allow investigators to decide which frequency and confidence combinations meet their criteria for counting.**

The [confidence standard](../confidence-standard.md) is the companion to this definition: it states how sure the project is that a recorded site meets the definition, how that confidence is scored, calibrated and validated, and under which standard version each recorded judgment was made. The [summary of AI assistance in the research pipeline](../ai-assisted-research.md) states which of those judgments models make and where people decide.

Counting follows from the recorded attributes and a study's declared rule.

Each study states its target population, observation times, evidence standard, and filters. That holds for our own studies, and it is how the record will serve groups whose interests and purposes differ from ours: the record holds the attributes and their imprecision, and a count is a study's derivation from them.

Again, we distinguish between the concepts of uncertainty and of vagueness. Usually we face uncertainty when judging a candidate PoW: the event has a sharp boundary and the sources fail to date it, so the record holds the earliest and latest supported dates and the estimate's coverage, as the decade rule under criterion 2 requires. Sometimes, however, we face vagueness: the event itself has no sharp boundary. When did the Cathedral of Mexico begin? Construction ran from 1573 to 1813, yet there is no clear day in those decades on which the site became a cathedral; its development was perhaps gradual and embryonic. Where there is vagueness we use our three criteria and give examples. The criteria remove the vagueness even as sources may leave uncertainty (ruling, 2026-09-22). Years with no evidence of activity yield "unknown" rather than confident zeros.

We note that a country is an index derived from location. A country, and data collected at country level by country-level authorities, are declared relational attributes. Each record carries the present-day country under its point, resolved from the location at entry and recoverable from the geometry at any later time. Where a study needs the polity a place stood in at a given date, that is a time-indexed claim recorded with the place's history, distinct from the index (recorded 2026-09-22).

## Revision note (0.1.6 → 0.1.7)

This revision follows the project lead's rulings of 2026-11-16. It makes five changes and otherwise leaves the rules of version 0.1.6 as they were.

First, mobile venues. The lead ruled that a vehicle or other mobile venue is not a site. Where worship by or for a community recurs at a stop the vehicle returns to, the stop is the site and may be a place of worship, and the vehicle is recorded as a linked entity, as an organisation is. A vehicle with no recurring stop yields no place of worship, however regular its worship, and its activity is evidence about the organisation that runs it. The rule follows from criterion 1: a vehicle's changing position gives investigators no fixed place to find again, whereas a stop it returns to does. The rule also treats a vehicle as the definition already treats an organisation, which conducts worship in many places without being a place itself. The rule is stated under criterion 1 and in the section on identity. The worked example of a Sukkah Mobile, a vehicle the lead photographed inviting passers-by to make the blessing on the lulav, shows both outcomes: a vehicle parked at the same corner through Sukkot each year yields one place with intermittent use, and a vehicle roaming with no fixed stop yields none. The visitors to the corner come separately, which the definition already counts as asynchronous community use of one site.

Second, the Christchurch example. Version 0.1.6 rules that a later recurrence of worship at a location, by a community without any succession to the earlier use, is a new place, and that a place ends only when its worship ceases with no community continuing or succeeding to its use. Its Christchurch example did not apply that rule to the land the Transitional Cathedral stands on, which the example called "another site". Public sources settle the facts. The foundation stone of St John the Baptist Church at Latimer Square, designed by Benjamin Mountfort and Maxwell Bury, was laid on 24 June 1864, and the church was consecrated on 27 December 1865 (Christchurch City Libraries, Heritage Photograph Collection). The church was demolished after the February 2011 earthquake, and the Transitional Cathedral, designed by Shigeru Ban, opened on its site in August 2013 (Christchurch City Libraries). In 2018 the diocese stated that the Latimer Square site and the Transitional Cathedral belong to the parish, and that the cathedral would use the building until the reinstatement in the Square is complete, after which the building would revert to the parish (*Christchurch Star*, 19 July 2018). Large majorities of St John's congregation voted in 2018 to leave the diocese (Anglican Taonga, 3 July 2018), and the diocesan synod of 2023 dissolved the parish of St John's Latimer Square and transferred its responsibilities to the cathedral (Anglican Diocese of Christchurch, synod record for 2022 to 2025). The cathedral's worship at Latimer Square therefore succeeds to the parish's use, by agreement from 2013 and by transfer from 2023. The example still counts two places, and the Latimer Square place now begins with St John's: the years from the demolition to the opening are an inactive period, the cardboard building is a new structure on a continuing site, and the cathedral congregation is a second community there, linked to the inactive cathedral in the Square. The section on identity gains the sentence the example needs: a move to a distinct property begins a new place only when the destination holds none, and a community that moves to a site already holding a place of worship, and succeeds to its use there, becomes a further community of that place. That sentence applies the succession rule of version 0.1.6 to a move and adds no new criterion. The example spells the cathedral's name "ChristChurch Cathedral", as the cathedral's own About page and Christchurch City Libraries give it; the reinstatement project writes "Christ Church Cathedral".

Third, acknowledgements. The definition gains a short Acknowledgements section naming those whose discussions during 2026 shaped it.

Fourth, the implementation status. The version history said that version 0.1.6's revision note lists "the implementation that now lags the definition", a statement true at adoption. Since 2026-10-19 the occupancy validator in `convex/lib/occupancies.ts` follows version 0.1.6: a move to a distinct property ends the period at its site and links the new place by `successor_site_id`, and a move within the site keeps the place. Review decisions take the identity bases of version 0.1.6 and refuse `relocation` on new decisions. The [human review and release design](human-review-and-release.md) records the reconciliation that remains. The status notes in the [counting discussion](pow-counting-and-complexes-discussion-2026-09-22.md) are corrected in the same change.

Fifth, community advice. Section 6 of [version 0.1.0](place-of-worship-definition-2026-08-27.md) called for community advice, where relevant, before general rulings were adopted for named boundary classes; later versions dropped that call. The project is at an early stage and has not yet been able to consult the communities whose places it classifies, so it rules on every community's sites by the same criteria. The criteria section now says so and states that each such ruling is provisional and open to revision on the advice of the community concerned. The commitment applies to all communities alike, which is why it is stated in general form rather than for particular classes of place.

No accepted identifier, observation, or derived state changes with this revision. A study that counted the Transitional Cathedral as a place founded in 2013 under version 0.1.6 states which version it used. Under version 0.1.7 the Latimer Square place carries St John's history, which changes that place's age, duration, and turnover, not the cross-sectional count.
