# Operational definition of a place of worship

**At a specified time, a place of worship is a reproducibly mappable site for which source evidence supports recurring religious worship by or for a community.**

Current version: 0.1.6. Adopted: 2026-09-22.

## Purpose and entity

A definition serves a purpose. This project asks what sites of collective worship do for the communities who express religious piety there, in one form or another, and through them for the societies around them. Those are functional questions. Answering a functional question requires consistent counting of such sites across places and times.

For our interests and purposes, a place of worship (PoW) is an entity described by six fundamental kinds of attribute. Spatial attributes indicate where it is and at what precision. Temporal attributes indicate when it existed, when worship recurred, and when it changed. Worship attributes indicate which recurring worship occurs and how often. Community attributes indicate by or for whom. Functional attributes indicate what the site does for that community, the matter the project exists to study. Relational attributes link a PoW to organisations and congregations, to the places of worship that preceded and succeeded it, and to the components of a "coherent worship complex". Every attribute has two kinds of imprecision, which the record keeps apart: uncertainty, where evidence about a matter of fact is incomplete, and vagueness, where the boundary of the concept itself is indistinct, as in how often worship must recur or where an entity begins and ends. Better evidence reduces uncertainty; a stated rule settles inherent vagueness.

A unique PoW identifier tracks the identity of a PoW through time. Attributes change yet this identifier may remain the same; the identifier changes only when the entity does. The section on identity states when we take this to happen, given our interests and purposes. In the [2026-09-22 discussion of counting places and coherent worship complexes](development/pow-counting-and-complexes-discussion-2026-09-22.md) we work through examples. Version 0.1.6 adopted that discussion's rule for replacement venues and shared rooms; the boundary of a "coherent worship complex" remains open in this linked discussion.

## Criteria

Again, recalling that uncertainty is permissible, we say a site meets our definition of a PoW when source evidence supports all of the following three criteria:

1. **Reproducibly mappable site.** Preserved location evidence allows investigators to identify the same physical place at the spatial precision required by the study. The site may be a building, parcel, compound, room, adapted or shared venue, temporary structure, or natural place.
2. **Recurring religious worship.** Evidence supports repeated or established worship use. Weekly services, seasonal practice, and an established annual pilgrimage may each qualify. Uncertain recurrence remains provisional. For the census-year derivation, a recorded period of worship at any known frequency establishes the place as in use for the years it covers, and each such year also carries a level of use: regular (weekly or more, monthly, or several times a year) or intermittent (annual or occasional). Only a period whose frequency of use is uncertain covers its years without establishing use; those years derive as uncertain until a reviewer decides on the evidence (rulings R-F1 and R-F1′, 2026-09-03). A period states whether it covers its years throughout or places worship somewhere within them: "the 1920s" may mean either, and only the first establishes each year, while the second derives every year of the decade as uncertain until narrowed (ruling, 2026-09-22).
3. **Worship by or for a community.** Worship occurs by or for a congregation, resident religious community, or defined institutional population. *For* admits worship conducted on a community's behalf: a chaplaincy serving a hospital, prison, or military population, or a ritual specialist performing worship for a community that does not assemble as a congregation. The criterion therefore does not presuppose that the community is the agent of worship. Restricted access is compatible with community use.

Private devotion, religious affiliation, sacred significance, religious architecture, an organisation's address, and a one-off ceremony are candidate evidence assessed against all three criteria. When we describe "evidence" we mean public evidence. Privacy, access, and cultural rules govern collection, storage, and publication. We respect privacy. That a place cannot meet our standard of reproducible evidence does not entail that it is not a community's place of worship. Our definition is relative to our interests and purposes, which are inevitably limited.

Two kinds of site call for a stated default: cemeteries and homes. Both enter the record as candidates from any source, and both become places of worship only when evidence shows recurring worship by or for a community there. Silence in the sources leaves them uncertain; the definition sets no default inclusion by tradition or by site type, since such a default would settle identity by the silent heuristic the project forbids. A household is not a community for criterion 3: a family altar or a household shrine is private devotion, however regular, and does not establish a place of worship. A corporate kin group worshipping at a shared site, a lineage, clan, gens, or hapū, is a community for criterion 3; the household exclusion reaches only the domestic unit worshipping for itself. A home counts when it hosts worship by or for a community beyond the household, as a house church does, or as a great house does when the district gathers there. A cemetery counts when recurring rites for a community occur there, at whatever frequency the evidence supports, and fails criterion 2 when it serves burial and secular commemoration alone (ruling, 2026-09-22).

The household exclusion is a decision about the unit, and the rationale should be on record. Fustel de Coulanges describes the religion of the ancient city as lodged first in the hearth cult of each household and then in the shared cults of the gens, the phratry, and the city. Hearth worship of that kind is religion, and it is socially consequential. It is excluded as a place because it is not a place in the project's sense: were every household with a shrine a site, the count of places of worship in a city would equal the count of its households and would measure population rather than the organisation of religion. What domestic worship is, for the project's questions, is a property of a population, the prevalence of worship at home in a community at a time. The shift Fustel describes, from a religion lodged in households to one lodged in shared sites, is measurable only if domestic worship is recorded at that level, as a documented attribute of a community or an area beside the count of its shared sites. Recorded as sites it would be invisible, drowned in its own denominator. Public, reproducible location evidence for private hearths does not exist for the ancient city and is forbidden to collect for living households; what the sources give is evidence about the institution, which the record can hold about a population and cannot hold as a site (ruling, 2026-09-22).

## Identity over time

We think of a place of worship as one entity at one site. A PoW's identifier follows the place of worship even as its attributes may change. For example it keeps its identifier through change in its fabric, its organisations, its communities, and its activity. A building burnt down and rebuilt, restored, enlarged, or moved within the site is, for us, the same place of worship. However we mark changes in its spatial and other attributes over time. The site is the unit at the spatial precision the study requires (criterion 1). A building shifted within its parcel or compound therefore stays within the same site. A building that is picked up and shipped overseas, and set up for worship in a new setting is not the same place of worship. Note there is inherent vagueness in what attributes count as change sufficient to call a PoW the same PoW over time. Recall that we are interested in consistent counting. Yet edge cases are inevitable. A PoW moved across five paddocks to a new owner and set up for another denomination might reasonably demand a new identifier. One moved the same distance to a new owner, but retaining its denomination, is a vague case that demands a rule. One that is not moved but undergoes such a change in ownership and denomination might also be considered a vague case in need of a rule. Generally ruling on vague cases would not affect counts of PoWs in a region but might have implications for longitudinal analyses. For us, a change of religion, denomination, congregation, or organisation while worship continues at the site is dated as a change in its community attributes. For us, inactivity is an attribute too: a place whose worship has ceased while its site stands and no other use has claimed it is inactive, keeps its identifier, and switches back to active if worship resumes; otherwise it remains dormant. We expect that investigators will use worship recurrence rates (and their confidence levels) as the relevant functional indicators for a PoW. Having a site identifier is not sufficient for thinking a PoW remains active. However, it is sufficient for thinking that, according to the evidence as we judged it, the site was at some time used by or for a community for recurring worship.

For us, a place of worship ends (and is not merely dormant) when its recurring worship ceases and the site is destroyed, repurposed, or abandoned, with no community continuing or succeeding to its worship use there. Whether a long gap is an inactive period or an end is a reviewer's recorded judgement on the evidence, with the basis and the alternative kept. The definition does not set a duration threshold. For our purposes, a later recurrence of worship at the same location, by a community without any succession to the earlier use, is a new place of worship with its own identifier, linked to the earlier one by location (think of the Cathedral of Mexico, built over the Templo Mayor). Hence, for us, location alone is never sufficient to establish identity: the ground is a spatial attribute, and a place of worship is more than its ground (ruling, 2026-09-22).

For us, a move to a different site initiates a new place of worship, noting again the inherent vagueness of "move." A site that is lifted and shuffled a few metres (or across five paddocks) serving the same community would not generally count as a new site. A place moved across town might well do. In such a case, the place at the new site would have a unique identifier, linked to its predecessor as a relational attribute, and the entity at the old site, if one indeed remained there, would stay in the record, recorded as inactive or ended (ruling, 2026-09-22). The community's continuity lives in the link; the identifier stays with the site. Paddock shuffling within the same parcel aside, we rule that any move to a distinct property requires a unique identifier.

A place of worship is neither a building nor an organisation. An organisation may conduct worship in many places; each mappable place of collective worship is considered its own unique unit; organisations and congregations are recorded as linked entities. A building or compound may hold several places of worship, each at its own site within it. Think of a hospital with a chapel and a separate prayer room. A room shared by several communities, at the same hours or at different ones, would on our pragmatic definition count as a single place of worship with several community attributes.

To count as a PoW, there must at some point in time be evidence (with uncertainty) for recurring collective worship. On religionmap.org at present, most PoW information has been sourced from OpenStreetMap. This indicates that someone thought, perhaps erroneously, that a location had the OpenStreetMap attribute for place of worship, which is ill defined. As such the evidence for religionmap.org at present is, taken as a whole, uncertain. Moreover, at any given time, whether worship at a location continues at an earlier place of worship is an inference from evidence about the site, the community, and its practice. OpenStreetMap gives uncertain and inconsistent evidence for temporal bounds.

**Our project therefore records identity as an explicit, revisable review decision. Our review systems require a stated basis for decisions about the attributes claimed for the locations on the map.**

### A comment on geography

We use the term "mappable" to denote geography. However, there are two complications. The geography is typically uncertain for historic places. Streets are built or renamed. Towns empty. Civilisations fall to ruins and others replace them. However, fortunately, longitude and latitude remain constant. To handle spatial uncertainty we allow a place to have spatial uncertainty bands around any point, as well as temporal uncertainty bands (which we have yet to figure out how to visually represent, even as the data are collected).

We note further that a point may not be precise enough. Within one plotted point we count one place of worship per distinct worship space, unless the spaces are components of one coherent worship complex, in which case the complex counts once. Two communities with their own rooms are the ordinary evidence that there are two spaces; one room used by two communities is one space with two community attributes. So two distinct hospital chapels known only to the same hospital centroid remain two places of worship drawn at the same point. We say that identity requires evidence about the places the point represents (recorded 2026-09-22). The rationale is not that this carves nature at a joint. It is our interest and purpose in tracking social consequences: the activity of sharing a place of worship differs from the activity of keeping separate ones, and the count should register that difference.

### Worked examples

Here we offer some worked examples to clarify the (inevitably arbitrary) rules that govern vague cases, recalling that uncertainty requires a distinct parameter for each attribute. Each example states the arrangement, the count of identifiers, and the rule that supports our classification. These are the cases reviewers will encounter, and the cases most often counted wrongly.

- **Christ Church Cathedral and the Transitional Cathedral, Christchurch.** Two. The cathedral is one place of worship, inactive since the 2011 earthquakes, active again if its community returns after the reinstatement, and ended if they never return or the site is destroyed. The Transitional Cathedral, opened in 2013 on another site and known as the Cardboard Cathedral, is a second place with its own identifier, linked to the first. Both count while both are in use, and the second counts for as long as worship continues there, whether or not the first reopens.
- **Hagia Sophia, Istanbul.** One. A church from the fourth century, a Latin church from 1204 to 1261, Orthodox again to 1453, a mosque to 1935, a museum to 2020, and a mosque since. Each conversion continued worship at the site under a new community and is a dated change of community and worship attributes; the museum years are an inactive period; one identifier throughout.
- **The Templo Mayor and the Metropolitan Cathedral, Mexico City.** Two. The Templo Mayor was enlarged and rebuilt over its earlier stages some seven times between the fourteenth century and 1521: one place of worship with dated structures, orientations, and rituals. Its worship ended with its destruction after the conquest. The cathedral, begun in 1573 within the Aztec sacred precinct and built in part from the temple's stone, is a new place of worship with no succession to the earlier use, born across the decades of its construction and dated as an interval. The reused fabric and the shared ground are relational attributes, and neither makes the two places one.
- **A building moved within its site.** One. A church shifted within its parcel is the same place of worship; the move is a dated spatial attribute.
- **A room that passes from Catholic to shared use.** One. The change of community is dated; the identifier stays.
- **A hospital prayer room shared by Muslims and Catholics, at the same hours or at different ones.** One, multi-faith, with the communities, rituals, and schedules as attributes. Private prayer and last rites conducted there are worship attributes of that place. An isolated bedside rite elsewhere in the hospital is evidence of activity to assess against criterion 2, and by itself no place.
- **A hospital with a distinct Catholic chapel and a distinct Muslim prayer room.** Two. Each room is its own site within the building; the hospital's roof and ownership unite nothing.
- **A cathedral whose nave serves the diocese and whose side chapel serves a Polish congregation.** One. Both spaces are components of the cathedral, and the second community is a community attribute of the complex. The same two communities in a hospital's separate chapel and prayer room count two, because there the spaces are separately established inside a facility whose purpose is not worship.
- **A hospital chaplaincy suite with a chapel and a prayer room off one lobby, run as one spiritual-care centre.** One. The spaces are organised together as a worship precinct, so they form a coherent worship complex within the hospital, with two community attributes. This is the boundary case a hospital reviewer meets first, and the judgement is recorded with its basis.
- **A cathedral with several altars, a convent with several worship rooms, a worship campus with several buildings, or three adjacent temple buildings forming one coherent worship complex.** One each. The components and their differing rituals and recurrence are attributes of the complex, recorded when the source distinguishes them and never required at entry. Recorded component activity keeps its scope and is never summed, since one ceremony can appear under several components; the complex counts at most once at an observation time.
- **Two adjacent, independently established places of worship.** Two. Adjacency, shared ownership, and a common roof establish no complex. Where a coherent complex ends remains open, and the [counting discussion](development/pow-counting-and-complexes-discussion-2026-09-22.md) works through it.
- **Two distinct hospital chapels known only to the same hospital centroid.** Two, drawn at one point, as above.
- **A municipal cemetery where families keep Obon, Qingming, All Souls or Día de Muertos rites.** One, with intermittent use. The rites recur on a calendar the community keeps, so the cemetery meets criteria 2 and 3 and derives present at the intermittent level under R-F1′; each study's threshold decides whether it counts. A cemetery used only for burial and secular commemoration is no place of worship. Rites such as mizuko kuyō, performed at a temple by individuals visiting separately over time, count through the temple as asynchronous community use of one site.
- **A churchyard or a temple cemetery.** None of its own. The burial ground is a component of the coherent worship complex and never counts separately.
- **A home.** One when it is a house church: an apartment where a congregation meets, or a room used weekly by a fellowship, with the identifier attached to the dwelling as a site and privacy rules governing publication. None when it holds a family altar or a household shrine, because the household is not a community for criterion 3, whatever the regularity of its devotion.
- **An ancestral hall, a lineage's citang at Qingming, a gens' tomb at the Parentalia, a marae's urupā.** One each. A corporate kin group beyond one household is a community for criterion 3, the rites recur, and the site is mappable. A reviewer who reads "household" as reaching these has misread the rule.
- **The household hearth: a lararium, a butsudan, a Hindu home shrine.** None as a site. Where documented for a population, domestic worship is recorded as an attribute of that community or area, so that the balance between worship at home and worship at shared sites can be studied. A household so large that it is a community, a Roman familia with its clients, a joint-family compound of dozens, is judged by whether worship there is by or for people beyond the residents: if the district gathers, it is a place; if the compound worships for itself, its cult is recorded as a practice of that community.

## Measurement over time

The analytical unit is a place of worship's time-indexed **worship-function state**. Record physical existence, worship use, location, organisations, congregations, timing, and uncertainty as distinct claims. The `site_id` identifies the place of worship as an entity; dated occupancy records give the years its site was in use, and geometry history dates changes within the site.

Source records, OpenStreetMap objects, and field observations provide evidence about the worship-function state. Reviewer acceptance establishes supported claims as recorded states and longitudinal events. An accepted record may preserve a time-bounded history after worship use ends. Preserve the source or community description alongside any project classification.

**Our purpose here is to consistently record evidence for attributes of PoWs**.

**We allow investigators to decide which frequency and confidence combinations meet their criteria for counting.**

Counting follows from the recorded attributes and a study's declared rule.

Each study states its target population, observation times, evidence standard, and filters. That holds for our own studies, and it is how the record will serve groups whose interests and purposes differ from ours: the record holds the attributes and their imprecision, and a count is a study's derivation from them.

Temporal bounds are approached from two angles, and the record keeps them apart. Usually we face uncertainty alone: the event has a sharp boundary and the sources fail to date it, so the record holds the earliest and latest supported dates and the estimate's coverage, as the decade rule under criterion 2 requires. Sometimes we face vagueness: the event itself has no sharp boundary. When did the Cathedral of Mexico begin? Construction ran from 1573 to 1813, and no day in those decades is the day it became a cathedral. Where there is vagueness we apply a criterion. A place of worship begins when recurring worship by or for a community begins at the site, and ends by the rule under Identity over time; building, consecration, and completion are dated attributes, and the beginning is the first of them at which the criterion holds. The criterion removes the vagueness and leaves the uncertainty where it was: the sources may still fail to say when recurring worship began in the unfinished cathedral, and the record then holds the bounds the evidence supports (ruling, 2026-09-22).

A country is an index derived from location. A country, and data collected at country level by country-level authorities, are declared relational attributes. Each record carries the present-day country under its point, resolved from the location at entry and recoverable from the geometry at any later time. Where a study needs the polity a place stood in at a given date, that is a time-indexed claim recorded with the place's history, distinct from the index (recorded 2026-09-23).
