The project in eight parts

What exists today, what each part is for, and where to try it. Cards with a blue edge changed this week.

Public map

religionmap.org and 100 country pages live

The public map shows every place of worship recorded in OpenStreetMap, with census religious-affiliation overlays for the countries where we hold them and a time slider over reviewed occupancy data where it exists. Each country page has its own census note and sources.

Contribute

Every place is revisable, from any page live

Clicking a place offers Revise this place. The Contribute entry on the map and on every country page offers three ways in: improve OpenStreetMap (anyone), submit evidence to religionmap.org (project members), and review evidence (reviewers). The project entry names the country under the map, so a member looking at Suva is offered the Fiji portal.

Fiji portal Research-assistant guide

Evidence portal

Add or revise a place, and say when it was used live

A member records what they can confirm at the observation date, then one card per period of worship use: how each date is known, how often the place was used, and where it stood. The function chain records what the place was and each change, including desacralisation. Every add or revise asks how sure the contributor is of the location and stores a radius. Sources go into a shared register, and the OpenStreetMap edit history of the record is shown as context.

New Zealand portal

Photographs and documents

Image and document evidence, held privately live

A contributor can attach photographs and documents to any add or revise as its second step. The files are stored in the project's own object store behind sign-in, opened through short-lived links, and never appear on the public map. Reviewers see them beside the record they support.

Attachments open inside the portal after sign-in.

Cases

Every submission is a case that opens and closes live

A nomination, a revision, or an issue report opens a task. The task moves through needs review, changes requested or an unresolved note, provisionally closed, reviewed, and exported. A contributor may withdraw a draft, and the withdrawal stays on the record. A reviewer may return a task with a question, and the answer comes back on the same task.

New Zealand review portal

Backend

A record of every change, with nothing overwritten live

The portal writes to a Convex database. Each save is a new version with a pointer to the version it revises, and superseded versions stay. Reviewer decisions are stored with a note; derived census-year states are stored with the rule that produced them and an event trail; exports are batches that can be compared as sets of record hashes. The result is a history of who asserted what, when, and on what evidence.

Backend function reference

Review layer

Claim, decide, confirm, or send back live

Reviewers work from a claim pool, cannot review their own submissions, and may request a second or third opinion. They confirm, override, or reject each derived census-year state and denomination, see the location grade and any pin movement, and read the attachments and the OpenStreetMap history. Every country has a review lane. A capped, provider-neutral AI-assisted review pilot is approved for comparison against independent human decisions.

Data and definition

The OSM year, the hashing lane, and definition 0.1.5 hashing next

We counted every OpenStreetMap place of worship by country at two anchors and report the change below. The next lane stores each annual pull as a hashed edition, so that the following year's pull can be compared with it record by record. The operational definition reached version 0.1.5; every version is kept as an immutable snapshot so a study can cite the rule it used.

The OSM year: what changed between two anchors

Every OpenStreetMap node or way tagged as a place of worship, counted by country at 1 September 2025 and at 27 July 2026.

On 3 September we counted OpenStreetMap places of worship for 177 country units at two moments: 1 September 2025, the project's annual anchor, and 27 July 2026, the latest state the ohsome history service then held. The world total rose from 1,485,702 to 1,544,023, a net gain of 58,321 records, or 3.9 percent in eleven months. 166 units grew, 8 were unchanged, and 3 fell by fewer than ten records each (Hungary, Luxembourg, Haiti).

Half of the net growth came from ten countries. The United States added the most records but grew slowly, at 1.8 percent. Indonesia, India and Brazil each added three to five thousand records, at six to seven percent. China grew by 12.6 percent. The fastest movers were not the largest: Papua New Guinea rose by 45 percent, from 1,768 to 2,572 records, and Kazakhstan, Guinea, Venezuela, Cambodia and Vietnam each rose by 15 to 20 percent. New Zealand, by contrast, added 26 records to 3,298, under one percent.

The reading is straightforward, and it sets how we should use the map. A 45 percent rise in Papua New Guinea in eleven months is mapping activity, not a 45 percent rise in places of worship. The count measures how completely OpenStreetMap has caught up with the world, and the countries growing fastest are the countries where the map was least complete. Where the map is mature, as in New Zealand or Germany, the count is nearly flat and the remaining change is the ordinary churn of edits. The net figure also hides its parts: a country that added 100 records and removed 100 shows no change at all. That is the limit of counting, and the reason for the hashing lane.

Boundaries are Natural Earth 1:110m polygons, which clip coasts and small islands: Vanuatu counts 29 here against 214 in the project's own extract. Relations are excluded. Use the table for scale and ranking. The full table of 177 units is in the repository at osm-pow-counts-2026-09-03.md.

Counting is where we start, not where we stop. We are moving forward on the hashing lane, which turns the annual pull into an edition: each country's records are stored at the 1 September anchor with a SHA-256 hash per file and a version identifier, validated against the project's manifest schema and kept in durable object storage. The following year's edition is then compared with it record by record, and each difference is labelled by class: stable, tag change, geometry change, apparent addition, apparent removal, duplicate split or merge. New Zealand, which already has a year-by-year pull from 2013, is the first edition; the runner then generalises to every country with exact boundaries, one country per request, resumable. The same discipline applies inside the project's own data: an export is a batch of record hashes, and two exports are compared as sets.

This week, in one list

Forty-one pull requests merged between 28 August and 3 September, grouped by what they changed.

  • The portal for every country. A world registry of 198 countries; the portal opens for any of them, draws today's OpenStreetMap places from the map tiles where no country product exists, and the review portal uses the same registry.
  • Contribute everywhere. The Contribute entry on the global map and all 100 country pages; Revise this place in every popup; signed-out visitors can look, and Sign in to revise this place remembers their choice.
  • Time. Periods of use with how each date is known; the function chain with desacralisation and resumed worship; census-year presence and denomination derived on the server and confirmed by a reviewer; a frequency-of-use threshold in the definition.
  • Location. An uncertainty radius in every add-place flow, converted to a grade (parcel, street, locality, area); OpenStreetMap edit history as context; revising a record collects the same evidence as adding one, attachments included.
  • Review. The claim pool with author exclusion and second opinions; return-for-comment; confirm, override, or reject on every derived year; the withdraw action with its audit record.
  • Data in. Robert Woodberry's Vanuatu mission stations, 80 places across the Catholic and Protestant atlases, imported into the review queue with their periods.
  • The definition from 0.1.2 to 0.1.5: the rationale for worship "by or for" a community; a place of worship as a continuant that survives relocation; the frequency threshold, amended so that an annual service is certain knowledge of rare use rather than uncertainty.
  • Design. An audit of the three surfaces with a decision set, ruled on 4 September; the design pass is in review (see below).

The screens

Six captures from this week's walkthroughs, before the design pass.

The design pass, and your eye

The screens above show the problem as well as the progress. Three surfaces written at different times disagree on colour, type size, and button style. An audit this week counted about 120 distinct colours in the portal stylesheet and found amber doing seven jobs at once; text that research assistants read for hours is set at caption size; and several controls fall below the contrast that accessibility guidelines require. Joseph B has ruled on the policy questions, and a pass that reduces the three surfaces to one system of tokens is in review. Three decisions in particular deserve a second opinion from the team:

  • Unvalidated places (today's OpenStreetMap points that nobody has yet verified) change from amber to a small slate-grey disc with a white halo, so that amber can mean only "needs attention": uncertain, disputed, or a caution.
  • On phones, Contribute joins the bottom row of actions and offers only the link to the portal, since the OpenStreetMap editor does not work on a phone.
  • Priority leaves colour altogether and becomes a word in the task row, because a red-green priority dot beside a green status pill cannot be told apart by readers with the commonest colour-vision deficiency.

The full audit and decision set is in the repository at docs/development/ui-design-audit-2026-09-03.md.

Where we most want your feedback

  1. The observation options. Are the four options in the add-place form (in use; exists but use uncertain; exists but not used; no longer exists) the right partition for what you can observe in the field or in a source? Is any case missing or ambiguous?
  2. Periods and the function chain. Does one card per period, with the function chain beneath, match how your sources describe a place's history? Is there a kind of change the chain cannot express?
  3. Derived census-year states. The server derives present, absent, or uncertain for each census year from the periods. Would you trust a derived state you had not entered yourself, given that a reviewer must confirm it? Would you want to see the rule that produced it?
  4. The unvalidated dots. Every country's portal now shows today's OpenStreetMap places as revisable dots. Is that the right promise to make to a new research assistant, or does it invite revision of places we know nothing about?
  5. The OSM year. Is the class vocabulary for the record-by-record comparison (stable, tag change, geometry change, apparent addition, apparent removal, duplicate split or merge) the right set for the questions you would ask of a census of change?
  6. Review and cases. Is the claim pool, with second opinions and return-for-comment, enough for the review you expect to do? Are the case states the right ones?
  7. Design. Anything about colour, type size, wording, or layout, on any screen, on any device.
